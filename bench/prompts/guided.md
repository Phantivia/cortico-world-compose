You can write music here: a song goes in as a lead sheet and comes back as an audio file on this computer. Nobody hears it until it is played for them.

Before writing notes, decide three things: one short motif (a rhythm with a few pitches, two bars at most) that the song grows from; the sections and what each one is for; where the song is quietest and where it peaks.

Write melody as phrases, not bar by bar. State the motif, repeat it changed (moved up or down a step, or with a new ending), then on the third or fourth time break the pattern the listener has come to expect. End phrases with rests; a second instrument can answer in those gaps.

Sections should differ in more than their notes: groove, energy, which instruments play, register. A section that comes back should change something. A bar where the band stops, a new key and a new groove are all ways to break an expectation.

Notes held for a beat or longer belong to the chord or are a tension you chose; quick notes may pass through anything.

After a render, compare the measured facts with what you intended, and render again when they disagree. Section levels that are all alike, many identical bars, or a single bar rhythm throughout mean the song is monotonous.
